# Glossary — Phase 3 (Songwriting & Arrangement)

## Song form
The order of sections in a song, written as letters or labels (for example V-C-V-C-B-C). Form is the map; sections are the rooms.

## Section
A block of a song with its own job and usually its own chords, melody and energy: intro, verse, pre-chorus, chorus, bridge, outro.

## Verse
The section that tells the story. Verses usually share the same music but change the lyric, sit lower and calmer than the chorus, and lead towards it.

## Pre-chorus
A short section (often 2–4 bars) between verse and chorus that builds tension, often by ending on the V chord or climbing in pitch.

## Chorus
The high point that repeats with the same music and words. It usually contains the hook, sits higher, and has the fullest arrangement.

## Bridge
A contrasting section, heard once, usually after the second chorus. It changes chords, rhythm or key so that the final chorus feels fresh.

## Intro
The opening bars before the first verse. Often built from chorus or verse chords with fewer instruments.

## Outro
The ending section. It either fades a repeating chorus or ends on a clear cadence to I.

## AABA
A 32-bar form of four 8-bar sections: a main melody (A) heard twice, a contrasting bridge (B), and a return of A. Common in older pop and jazz standards.

## Hook
The most memorable short idea in a song — usually a melodic phrase in the chorus, but it can be a riff, a rhythm or a sound.

## Motif
A short musical idea (2–6 notes, often one bar) with a recognisable rhythm and shape that the rest of a melody repeats and varies.

## Melodic sequence
Repeating a motif starting on a different scale degree, usually one step higher or lower, keeping its shape.

## Question and answer
Two phrases that belong together: the question ends open (often on degree 2, 5 or 7 over V), the answer starts the same way and ends closed on degree 1. Also called antecedent and consequent.

## Climax
The highest or most intense note of a melody. Good melodies save it for one place, usually late in the chorus.

## Root-fifth bass
A bass line that alternates the chord root with the fifth above or below it — the backbone of country, pop and folk bass.

## Walking bass
A bass line that moves mostly in steady quarter notes by step, landing on chord tones on strong beats and using passing notes in between.

## Approach note
A note one step (diatonic or chromatic) above or below the next chord's root, played just before the chord change to lead the ear into it.

## Kick lock
When the bass rhythm matches the kick drum hits, so the two sound like one instrument. The first thing to check in any groove.

## Backbeat
Snare hits on beats 2 and 4 in 4/4 — the heartbeat of most pop, rock and R&B.

## Four-on-the-floor
A kick drum on every quarter note (1, 2, 3, 4), typical of disco, house and much dance-pop.

## Ghost note
A very quiet snare hit between the main hits. You feel it more than hear it; it makes a groove bounce.

## Fill
A short break in the drum pattern (usually the last beat or two of a section) that signals "something new is coming".

## Velocity
How hard a MIDI note is struck (0–127, or 0–1 in the app). Higher velocity is louder and often brighter.

## Humanising
Adding small variations in velocity (and sometimes timing) so that programmed drums sound played rather than machine-stamped.

## Half-time
A groove whose snare falls only on beat 3, so the music feels half as fast while the tempo stays the same. Typical of trap and big choruses.

## Frequency roles
The idea that each layer should own a register: low (bass, kick), middle (chords, pads, vocal/lead), top (hi-hats, shimmer). Layers in the same register compete.

## Doubling
Playing the same line on two instruments, or in two octaves, to make it stronger and wider.

## Counter-melody
A second, quieter melody that moves when the main melody rests, usually in a different register.

## Comping
Playing chords rhythmically (short, repeated, syncopated hits) rather than holding them — piano and guitar comp, pads sustain.

## Pad
A sustained, soft chord sound that fills the middle of the arrangement without adding rhythm.

## Texture
How many layers are playing and how they relate: thin (one or two layers) or thick (many layers). Changing texture is the main way to change energy.

## Arrangement map
A table of sections (columns) against instruments (rows) showing who plays where. It is how you plan a textural build.

## Mode
A scale built by starting the major scale on a different degree, giving a new tonic and a new colour. Dorian, Phrygian, Lydian and Mixolydian are the most used in pop.

## Dorian
Natural minor with a raised 6th (for example D E F G A B C). Minor, but brighter and more hopeful. Typical chords: i and IV major.

## Mixolydian
Major with a flattened 7th (for example G A B C D E F). Major, but bluesy and relaxed. Typical chords: I and bVII.

## Lydian
Major with a raised 4th (for example F G A B C D E). Dreamy and floating. Typical chords: I and II major.

## Phrygian
Natural minor with a flattened 2nd (for example E F G A B C D). Dark and Spanish or metal-sounding. Typical chords: i and bII.

## Characteristic tone
The one note that makes a mode different from plain major or minor (the 6th in Dorian, the b7 in Mixolydian, the #4 in Lydian, the b2 in Phrygian). Put it on strong beats to make the mode audible.

## Pentatonic scale
A five-note scale without half steps. Major pentatonic is 1 2 3 5 6; minor pentatonic is 1 b3 4 5 b7. Almost impossible to play "wrong".

## Blues scale
Minor pentatonic plus the b5 (the blue note): 1 b3 4 b5 5 b7.

## Blue note
A note bent or placed between major and minor — mainly b3, b5 and b7 — that gives the blues its bittersweet sound.

## Twelve-bar blues
A 12-bar chord form: I I I I | IV IV I I | V IV I V (or I at the end). Usually all dominant 7th chords.

## Shuffle
A swung eighth-note feel where each beat is split long-short (like a triplet with the middle note missing).

## Riff
A short, repeated instrumental phrase (usually 1–2 bars) that carries a song, often in the bass or guitar register.

## Turnaround
The last one or two bars of a form (often V or V7) that lead back to the beginning.

## Call and response
A phrase ("call") answered by a different phrase or instrument ("response"). The foundation of blues and gospel.

## Secondary dominant
A dominant 7th chord borrowed to point at a chord other than I: V/V (D7 in C) resolves to V, V/vi (E7 in C) to vi, V/IV (C7 in C) to IV. It contains a note outside the key.

## Tonicisation
Briefly making a chord other than I feel like home by preceding it with its own dominant.

## Chromatic passing chord
A chord outside the key that connects two diatonic chords by half step in the bass, such as C#dim7 between C and Dm.

## Reharmonisation
Keeping a melody but changing the chords under it to give it a new colour.

## Song sketch
A rough 4–8 bar loop that captures the core idea (hook, progression or beat) before the song is structured and arranged.

## Seed
The first idea a song grows from: a hook, a chord progression or a beat.

## Mix
Balancing the finished tracks: relative volume (levels) and left-right position (pan) so every part can be heard.

## Pan
The left-right position of a track in the stereo image. Keep bass, kick, snare and lead in the centre; spread chords and percussion.
