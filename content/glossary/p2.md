# Glossary — Phase 2 (Harmony & Songs)

## Natural minor
The minor scale built with the step pattern W-H-W-W-H-W-W, written 1 2 b3 4 5 b6 b7. It uses the same notes as its relative major, starting from degree 6 (A natural minor = the white keys from A to A).

## Relative keys
A major key and a minor key that share the same notes and key signature, but have different home notes — for example C major and A minor.

## Relative minor
The minor key that shares a major key's notes. Its tonic is degree 6 of the major scale, three half steps below the major tonic (C major → A minor).

## Harmonic minor
Natural minor with degree 7 raised by a half step (1 2 b3 4 5 b6 7). The raised 7th gives the minor key a leading tone and a major V chord (E major in A minor).

## Melodic minor
Natural minor with degrees 6 and 7 raised when the melody goes up (1 2 b3 4 5 6 7); traditionally it returns to natural minor coming down. It smooths out the wide gap in harmonic minor.

## Tritone
The interval of six half steps (three whole tones), e.g. C–F# or B–F. It is exactly half an octave, sounds restless, and resolves inward or outward by half steps inside V7 → I.

## Interval inversion
Flipping an interval by moving its lower note up an octave (or its upper note down). The two intervals add up to 12 half steps: 3rds become 6ths, 2nds become 7ths, 4ths become 5ths, and major becomes minor.

## Harmonic interval
Two notes played at the same time, as opposed to a melodic interval, where they are played one after the other.

## Consonance
An interval or chord that sounds stable and blended. Octaves, fifths and fourths are perfect consonances; thirds and sixths are imperfect (sweet) consonances.

## Dissonance
An interval or chord that sounds tense or rough and wants to move — seconds, sevenths and the tritone. Dissonance isn't wrong: it creates the tension that makes resolution feel good.

## Compound interval
An interval wider than an octave, such as a 9th (octave + 2nd) or a 10th (octave + 3rd). Subtract 7 from the number to find the simple interval with the same colour.

## Bass clef
The F clef: its two dots surround the 4th line, which is F3. It notates low notes — left-hand piano, bass, cello. Middle C sits on one ledger line above it.

## Inversion
A chord voiced with a note other than the root at the bottom. First inversion has the 3rd in the bass (E G C); second inversion has the 5th in the bass (G C E). The chord and its root don't change.

## Voice leading
The way the individual notes of one chord move to the next. Smooth voice leading keeps common tones and moves the other notes by step, so chords connect without jumps.

## Seventh chord
A four-note chord: a triad plus the 7th above the root. The common types are major 7 (Cmaj7: C E G B), dominant 7 (C7: C E G Bb) and minor 7 (Cm7: C Eb G Bb).

## Dominant function
The role of V and V7 (and vii°): creating tension that pulls to the tonic. In V7 the tritone between the 3rd and 7th resolves by half steps into the tonic chord.

## Predominant
A chord that prepares the dominant, usually ii or IV. In ii – V – I, the ii chord is the predominant.

## Harmonic function
The job a chord does in a key: tonic (rest — I, vi, iii), subdominant or predominant (moving away — IV, ii) or dominant (tension — V, V7, vii°).

## Sixteenth note
A note lasting a quarter of a beat in 4/4 — four per beat, counted "1 e & a". Two sixteenths equal one eighth note.

## Syncopation
Accenting notes that fall between the beats (on an "&", "e" or "a"), often while leaving the following beat empty. It creates the forward push that makes rhythm feel groovy.

## Swing
Playing pairs of eighth notes long–short instead of evenly, roughly in a 2:1 triplet ratio. It is the basic feel of jazz, blues, shuffles and a lot of hip-hop.

## Chord tone
A melody note that belongs to the chord sounding underneath it (the root, 3rd, 5th, or 7th of a seventh chord). Chord tones sound stable, especially on strong beats.

## Non-chord tone
A melody note that is not in the current chord. The common kinds are passing tones (stepping between two chord tones), neighbour tones (stepping away and back) and suspensions (held over a chord change, then resolving down).

## Melodic contour
The overall shape of a melody's rise and fall — for example arch, ascending, descending or wave.

## Harmonic rhythm
How often the chords change: once every two bars, once per bar, twice per bar, and so on. Faster harmonic rhythm adds energy, especially approaching a cadence.

## Closely related keys
Keys whose signatures differ by at most one sharp or flat — neighbours on the circle of fifths, plus their relative minors. They share most of their chords, so moving between them sounds smooth.

## Parallel keys
A major and a minor key with the same tonic, such as C major and C minor. They share a home note but differ in three notes (3, 6 and 7).

## Borrowed chord
A chord taken from the parallel key, most often from the parallel minor into a major key — for example iv (Fm) or bVII (Bb) in C major. Also called modal mixture.

## Triplet
Three equal notes in the time normally taken by two. An eighth-note triplet puts three notes in one beat ("1-trip-let"); it is written with a small 3 over the notes, and as `8t` in the app. Swing is built from triplets.

## Compound meter
A meter whose beats divide into three instead of two. In 6/8 the six eighths of a bar are grouped 3 + 3, so there are two big beats (dotted quarters). Compare 3/4, whose six eighths are grouped 2 + 2 + 2 into three beats.

## Passing tone
A non-chord tone that steps between two different chord tones, usually on a weak beat: E – F – G over a C chord.

## Neighbour tone
A non-chord tone that steps away from a chord tone and back to it: E – F – E or E – D – E over a C chord.

## Suspended chord (sus chord, sus2, sus4)
A triad whose 3rd is replaced by the 4th (sus4: C F G) or the 2nd (sus2: C D G). With no 3rd it is neither major nor minor; sus4 sounds open and waiting and tends to fall back to the 3rd, sus2 sounds lighter and often doesn't resolve.

## Tension and resolution
Tension is a sound that feels unfinished and makes you expect something next (a dominant 7th, a suspension, a leading tone); resolution is the move that answers it, like V7 → I.

## Plagal cadence
The phrase ending IV → I: a soft "amen" arrival at home without dominant tension first.

## Deceptive cadence
The phrase ending V → vi: the dominant promises home, and the tonic-family vi arrives instead, so the music continues.
