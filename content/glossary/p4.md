# Glossary — Phase 4 (Composition Studio & Jazz)

## Extension
A chord tone stacked above the 7th: the 9th, 11th or 13th. They are the 2nd, 4th and 6th of the scale placed above the chord, and they add colour without changing the chord's function.

## Avoid note
A scale tone that clashes with a chord when held, usually because it sits a half step above a chord tone. The classic example is the natural 11 (F) over Cmaj7 or C7, which rubs against the 3rd (E).

## Add9 chord
A triad with an added 9th and no 7th, e.g. Cadd9 = C–E–G–D. Brighter than a triad but still fully stable.

## Sixth chord
A triad with an added major 6th: C6 = C–E–G–A, Cm6 = C–Eb–G–A. Used as a calm tonic when a maj7's leading tone would clash with the melody.

## Shell voicing
A three-note jazz voicing of a 7th chord: root, 3rd and 7th (as 1-3-7 or 1-7-3). It states the chord quality clearly and leaves room for a melody or soloist.

## Rootless voicing
A four-note voicing that omits the root (the bassist plays it) and adds a colour tone instead. The A-form is 3-5-7-9, the B-form 7-9-3-5; on dominant chords the 5th is often replaced by the 13th.

## Minor ii–V–i
The cadence of minor keys: a half-diminished ii chord (m7b5), a dominant V7 from harmonic minor, and a minor tonic (often m6). In A minor: Bm7b5 – E7 – Am6.

## 32-bar AABA
The classic song form of jazz standards: an 8-bar A section, its repeat, a contrasting 8-bar bridge (B), and a final A. One pass through all 32 bars is called a chorus.

## Rhythm changes
The chord progression of Gershwin's "I Got Rhythm" (1930), usually in Bb: A sections cycle I–vi–ii–V, and the bridge is a chain of dominants III7–VI7–II7–V7. Jazz players have written hundreds of new melodies over it.

## Jazz blues
The 12-bar blues enriched with ii–V progressions, a quick IV in bar 2, a passing diminished chord in bar 6 and an I–VI–ii–V turnaround in bars 11–12.

## Charleston rhythm
A comping figure: a chord on beat 1 and a short chord on the "and" of 2, then silence for the rest of the bar. Named after the 1920s dance.

## Modal interchange
Borrowing chords from a parallel key or mode (same tonic, different scale), most often from the parallel minor: in C major, Fm (iv), Eb (bIII), Ab (bVI), Bb (bVII) and Dm7b5 (iiø7).

## Tritone substitution
Replacing a dominant 7th chord with the dominant 7th a tritone away (Db7 for G7). Both share the same 3rd and 7th, so the tension is identical, but the bass now moves by half step.

## Passing diminished chord
A diminished 7th chord placed between two chords a whole step apart so the bass moves chromatically, e.g. Cmaj7 – C#dim7 – Dm7. It works like a rootless V7(b9) of the chord it leads to.

## Chord-scale
The scale that fits a particular chord: its chord tones plus the passing tones that sound good over it. Dm7 as ii takes D dorian, G7 as V takes G mixolydian, Cmaj7 as I takes C ionian or lydian.

## Guide tones
The 3rd and 7th of a chord, which define its quality. In a ii–V–I they form smooth lines moving by half step or common tone, so a melody built on them outlines the harmony.

## Enclosure
A melodic figure that surrounds a target note: a note above it, then a note below it (often chromatic), then the target itself on a strong beat. A signature sound of bebop.

## Jazz chorus
In jazz, one complete pass through a tune's form (e.g. all 12 bars of a blues or 32 bars of an AABA tune). Soloists "take a chorus" or several. Not the same as the chorus section of a pop song.

## Counterpoint
The art of combining two or more independent melodic lines so that each makes sense alone and they sound good together. Species counterpoint teaches it through strict exercises over a given melody.

## Cantus firmus
Latin for "fixed song": a slow given melody, usually in whole notes, against which a counterpoint is written in species exercises.

## Contrary motion
Two voices moving in opposite directions. The most independent kind of motion; the others are parallel, similar and oblique (one voice holds while the other moves).

## Parallel fifths
Two voices moving in the same direction while staying a perfect 5th apart (likewise parallel octaves). Avoided in counterpoint and part-writing because the voices fuse and lose independence; embraced in power chords for exactly that reason.

## ADSR
The four stages of a synth envelope: Attack (time to reach full level), Decay (time to fall to the sustain level), Sustain (level while the key is held) and Release (fade after the key is let go).

## Arpeggiator
A device or technique that plays the notes of a held chord one after another in a repeating pattern (up, down, up-down, random) at a fixed rhythm such as 16ths.

## Build-up
The section before a drop in electronic music that raises tension: accelerating snare rolls, rising lines, thinning textures and often a short silence at the end.

## Gating
Chopping a sustained sound rhythmically on and off. Leaving a gap where each kick hits imitates the "pumping" of sidechain compression.


## Odd meter
A time signature whose beats group unevenly, such as 5/4 (3+2) or 7/8 (2+2+3). Musicians feel it as a chain of 2s and 3s rather than counting every beat.

## Polyrhythm
Two or more different even pulses played at once, such as 3 against 2. Different from polymeter, where patterns of different lengths cycle against each other.

## Metric modulation
A tempo change made by reinterpreting a note value: e.g. the old triplet 8th becomes the new plain 8th, so ♩ = 80 becomes ♩ = 120 while the fastest pulse stays the same.

## Genre idiom
A habit so typical of a style that listeners recognise the style from it: the bVII chord and power-chord riffs in rock, fingerpicked arpeggios in folk, a repeated hook and pre-chorus lift in pop.

## 808
Short for the Roland TR-808 drum machine; in hip-hop and trap it means a deep, long, sine-like bass note that doubles as kick and bass line, usually starting with the kick drum.

## Chord stab
A short, punchy chord hit, usually on an off-beat, typical of house and funk. Often a triad placed over a different bass note to imply a 7th chord.

## Brightness ladder
The modes ordered from brightest to darkest, each one lowering a single note: lydian, ionian, mixolydian, dorian, aeolian, phrygian, locrian. Useful for choosing a mode to match a mood.

## Ostinato
A short musical figure repeated persistently, often while harmony, orchestration or dynamics change around it. Creates momentum, tension or hypnotic stillness.

## Leitmotif
A short theme associated with a character, place or idea, transformed (mode, tempo, orchestration) as the story develops. Popularised by Wagner and central to film scoring.

## Hit point
A precise moment in a film or game scene where the music must change or accent the action, calculated in bars and beats from the tempo.

## Through-composed
A form that keeps presenting new material instead of repeating whole sections (A B C D… rather than verse–chorus). Unity comes from developing motifs and from an overall tension arc.

## Prosody
The fit between the natural stress and rhythm of words and the melody they are set to. Good prosody puts stressed syllables on strong beats, longer notes or higher pitches.

## Portfolio
A small, varied collection of your best finished pieces, chosen to show range (e.g. one song, one groove-based track, one composed piece) and polished to a consistent standard.

## Transition
Whatever signals a change of section: a pickup, drop-out, drum fill, riser or harmonic setup (such as a V7) at the end of the old section.
