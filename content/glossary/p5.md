# Glossary — Phase 5 (Transcription & Mastery)

## Transcription
Working out by ear how a piece of recorded music is made — key, tempo, form, chords, bass, melody, groove and arrangement — and writing it down or rebuilding it in the DAW. In this course it is done in a fixed order of listening passes.

## Listening pass
One focused listen through a song (or a looped section) with a single question in mind, such as "where are the bass roots?". Transcription is a sequence of passes, not one heroic listen.

## Form map
A one-page chart of a song: one row per section with its name, length in bars, chords, and short notes on melody, groove and layers. It is the finished product of a transcription.

## Mystery song
An original multi-track example the app plays for you to decompose by ear, exactly as you would a song on the radio.

## Tonal centre
The note (and chord) a piece treats as home — degree 1. Find it by humming the most "finished" note and by checking the bass at the ends of phrases.

## Tap tempo
Measuring tempo by tapping along to the pulse and letting a tool (or a stopwatch and some arithmetic) turn the taps into BPM.


## Landing note
The note a bass line arrives on at a chord change, usually on beat 1. It is almost always the chord's root (or, in a slash chord, the named bass note); the notes between landings are connectors.

## Slash chord
A chord written with its bass note after a slash, e.g. G/B = G major with B in the bass. Used when the bass plays a chord tone other than the root (an inversion) or a note outside the chord.

## Pedal point
A bass note held or repeated while the chords above it change, e.g. C under C – F/C – G/C. It creates tension against the moving harmony.

## Reference track
A finished commercial recording you listen to on purpose — to study its form, harmony, groove or sound, or to compare your own mix against it. In this course reference tracks are analysed by ear from your own copy, never reproduced.

## Diatonic default
The quality a chord has when built only from the key's scale: in major, I IV V major, ii iii vi minor, vii° diminished. A transcriber predicts this quality from the root and listens only for departures from it.

## Suspension
A chord tone (usually the 3rd) temporarily replaced by the note above it, then resolved down. In chord symbols: sus4 (4th instead of 3rd) and sus2 (2nd instead of 3rd); pop often leaves sus2 unresolved as a colour.

## Anticipation
A melody (or chord) note that arrives slightly before the beat it belongs to — in pop typically an eighth note early, tied across the barline — giving the line a pushed, conversational feel.

## Skeleton-first
A dictation method: chunk the melody into short phrases, write only the first note of each bar (the skeleton, usually a chord tone), then the rhythm, then fill in the notes between.

## Layer map
A grid of sections (across) by instruments (down) showing which layers play where, with each layer's register and role (sustained or rhythmic). It is the arrangement pass of a transcription.

## Drop
In dance music, the section where the full beat, bass and hook land together after a build — the peak of the track's energy, usually signalled by a moment of silence just before it.

## Energy curve
A rough line drawn over a song's timeline showing how intense the arrangement feels in each section. Mapping it is the fastest way to hear the form of dance and pop tracks.

## Chord-family default
The extension a style usually adds to each chord family — in neo-soul, m9 on ii and vi, 13 or 9 on V, maj9 on I. Transcribers predict it, then listen only for departures.

## Pocket
The rhythmic lock between kick, snare and bass that makes a groove feel good — which notes the bass shares with the kick, which it places between them, and how everything sits against the backbeat.

## Power chord
A two-note chord of root and perfect 5th (often with the octave doubled), written with a 5: E5, C5. Having no 3rd, it is neither major nor minor; its function comes from the key and melody.

## Timebox
A fixed amount of time allotted to one stage of work; when it runs out you move on, finished or not. Timeboxing turns songwriting from open-ended polishing into a sequence of quick decisions.

## Topline
The main melody (and, in a song with lyrics, the vocal line) written on top of an existing beat or chord loop. "Toplining" is the beat-first way of writing a hook.

## Inversion numeral (figured bass)
A roman numeral with small numbers showing which chord tone is in the bass: V6 = first inversion (3rd in the bass, e.g.
G/B in C), V64 = second inversion (5th in the bass, G/D). Numerals never use a slash for this, because V/V means "the
dominant of V".

## Verdict first
The rule for analysing real songs in this course: listen and commit to your answers (key, tempo, form, bass, chords)
before reading any facts about the song, then compare.

## Form by layers
Building a song's sections by muting and adding layers over an unchanging loop instead of writing new chords — common in
dance, hip-hop and beat-first pop.
